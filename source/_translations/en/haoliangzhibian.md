---
title: "Zhuangzi - The Debate on the Bridge over the Hao River"
---

> Zhuangzi and Huizi were strolling on the bridge over the Hao River. Zhuangzi said, "The minnows swim about so freely and at ease; this is the happiness of fish." Huizi said, "You are not a fish, so how do you know the happiness of fish?" Zhuangzi said, "You are not me, so how do you know I don't know the happiness of fish?" Huizi said, "I am not you, so of course I don't know you; and you are certainly not a fish, so your not knowing the happiness of fish is settled." Zhuangzi said, "Let's go back to where we started. When you said 'how do you know the happiness of fish', you already knew that I knew it, and that's why you asked me. I knew it from here, above the Hao."

We've all heard the "you are not a fish" story, but we may never have really looked into what the story is actually about or what it means, and just took it superficially as "you're not a fish, so what makes you think you know the happiness of fish?" Extend that a bit and you get how a certain classmate understood it today when she told me "you're not me" and then blocked me. The background: she said she wanted to "run" abroad, and I told her that going abroad won't necessarily solve all your problems, and won't necessarily make you happier than staying in China. My reasoning was that she used to be a "little pink" (a young nationalist), and her parents are both civil servants who made money off government policy and own lots of apartments in China. Anyways, she was so hot-tempered she didn't give me a chance to keep debating, but I find "you're not me" an interesting thing to say. If the argument had continued, it would have turned into the Hao River debate, and I'd have used Zhuangzi's line: you're not me, so how do you know I don't understand your problem? More broadly, how can one person know another person's joys and sorrows, and how can one transcend the self to understand the outside world? From the standpoint of logic, Zhuangzi's argument is indeed subjective inference and can't rigorously reach the conclusion that the fish are happy. But thinking about it from the angle of empathy theory, Zhuangzi ultimately won the debate (of course, the story is also recorded in the *Zhuangzi*), by using linguistic ambiguity to shift philosophical positions, an epistemology built on empathy and intuition<sup>1</sup>. But from Zhuangzi's point of view, happiness is more a state of the heart; it doesn't need to be interrogated step by step for objective proof of validity. It is instead a free and unfettered kind of "knowing"<sup>2</sup>
