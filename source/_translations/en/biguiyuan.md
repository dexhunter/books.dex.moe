---
title: "My 1000 Days at Country Garden"
---

I wanted to read this book because I've recently gotten interested in biographies, and this one involves a name: "Country Garden". I first learned about Country Garden when I saw their Future Leaders program on a Chinese WeChat official account run by people at the University of Edinburgh. The program sounded pretty interesting, because right off the bat they said they were going to invest 20 billion yuan in industrial robotics (if I remember correctly). That's quite attractive to master's/PhD students, since you'd think 20 billion is enough to prop up the development of the whole industry. Although Country Garden is a real estate company, real estate has been getting saturated in recent years, so it's no surprise they started looking to diversify their investments. OK, back to the book. My impression of real estate was that it doesn't require any special intellectual ability; as long as you can sell, you're fine, and anyone who got in a few years earlier is financially free by now. Of course, because the barrier to entry in real estate is so low, lots of people do it, but very few get as big as Country Garden. Rumor online says this book contains confidential stuff, but after reading it all the way through, I just felt the author didn't really spill anything special. It feels like he had been inside the system for too long, or maybe it's just Eastern culture: he doesn't criticize directly, but voices his thoughts with some reservation, and he never forgets to praise or commend his colleagues and Country Garden. Since the author did eventually resign, logically, if he had been happy with the job he probably wouldn't have left, and the fact that he left suggests he wasn't very satisfied with it.

As for the actual content, I did learn some things. Because the author has a finance background, I mainly got a rough idea of finance and fundraising at large companies, such as credit ratings, financing, asset securitization, going public, and so on. I also learned how important things like the debt ratio and net profit are to a company, how capital operations work, and Country Garden's unique sales model and philosophy (Walmart-style marketing). There's also stuff on the role of investment banks, debt ratios, future growth rates, overseas investment, and so on.

The author praises Yang Guoqiang highly, and there's a lot worth learning from him. For example, paying attention to human needs, sharing success, sharing with one heart, and so on.

Of course, some parts gave me special inspiration. For example, most resources are held by a small number of people or companies, and this is a kind of consensus in human society (at least for now), so a fully decentralized organization is very hard to achieve. Second, the book has a lot of lessons about how to conduct yourself. I already knew most of them and agree with them, but there's still a gap between knowing and agreeing and actually doing. In real life, everyone only sees the results, so what the process was like is hard for people to say, and few people care. But I think the process is also very important.

At the end, the author voices some "complaints" or opinions, for example that there are too many incentive schemes, a dazzling array of them, and he felt they needed to be cut back, but in the end it fizzled out due to conflicts of interest among various parties. That's probably just an internal company problem. I think a big company must have a strong leader, with professional managers carrying out the leader's ideas at the execution level. That's very efficient, but in practice running and managing a company is still very complicated, the interests of every party may need to be considered, and sensible incentive schemes are still essential.

Of course, since I'm also feeling pretty lost about the future right now, I'm optimistic about several fields, but if I really want to accomplish something I'll definitely need to dig deep into one field. So I also hope the field I choose is the direction of the future, rather than one that's gradually being phased out.
