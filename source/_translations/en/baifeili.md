This is probably the most interesting novel I've read this year. I think this kind of novel is what really deserves to be called magic realism, because a lot of the little anecdotes, or stories, in it are things I've lived through or seen myself, so it's easy for me to relate. If I had to pin down a theme, I'd say the novel is about the current state of education in China. Fiction comes from life but rises above it, and I imagine the author is writing about things he went through or observed. The main thread follows the protagonist Bai Feili (白费力, "wasted effort"), who starts freshman year aiming for Tsinghua, then decides Tsinghua is nothing special and he's going for Stanford instead, because that way he isn't just following the crowd and looks more impressive. In the end, he fails both the recommended-admission route and the entrance exam for grad school at his own university, has no luck finding a job, and only gets into a master's program at a 211 university through connections. The story along the way is twisty and bizarre. There are a lot of fun passages, and I want to talk about some of them alongside what I've seen and heard myself.

> That summer after senior year of high school, Bai Feili told everyone he met that he'd had the bad luck of catching a terrible cold during the gaokao, and on top of that the exam room he was assigned to happened to have air conditioning, cranked down to a very low temperature, with the doors and windows shut tight.
> In reality, though, Bai Feili didn't have a bad cold on gaokao day at all. He was actually in unusually good shape, and he even ran into several questions he had reviewed. Getting that score was, as his classmates said, already a performance above his usual level. After all, his regular grades were right there, and they don't lie.

My gaokao went about the same as the protagonist's: a normal performance, maybe slightly above normal. I think that's how it goes for most people, or rather, the few classmates who supposedly "underperformed" only leave everyone else staring helplessly. `Three of them looked at him coldly and said, "Then go to a cram school and retake it! Come back next year as the top science scorer in the province"` I think a lot of people have had a similar experience: after the gaokao you think, maybe I should retake it. Most ordinary people aim higher than they can reach and feel that what they showed is far below their real ability, so they fight another year under the banner of "retaking." Of course, results vary from person to person, but simply put, I think if you didn't even make the first-tier cutoff the first time, retaking is useful. If you were already above the first-tier line, retaking won't necessarily help. In other words, going from 0 to 80 is easy, but going from 80 to 100 is much harder. So in the end I went to an ordinary first-tier university that isn't 985 or 211 (though it is a Sino-foreign joint venture). At first I thought about going to Anhui University or the Tongling campus of Hefei University of Technology, but the reality was my score wasn't high enough, so I'd have ended up in a really bad major. In the end my dad said the family would spend a bit more money so I could go to the joint-venture school. Even earlier, though (in my second year of high school), I always assumed I could take the SAT and go straight to the US for college. Later, first because my family's finances took a hit, and second because I didn't work hard to fight for it, I gave up on that (looking back now, I've given up on opportunities many times, and only occasionally did someone help me out so I could seize a chance).

> Erniu said, well, that's why his head doesn't work right. Even if Tsinghua students do go to grad school, would they choose to stay at their own school? Of course not, they'd all go abroad!

That's just the reality in mainland China: homegrown PhDs and master's degrees can't match a degree from Europe or America. Of course, when I went to Singapore I found it's the same there too. An American degree is basically recognized everywhere in the world, while degrees from many other countries are at most recognized at home.

> Actually Bai Feili took himself way too seriously. His business was at most something for others to laugh about when they were bored. There were so many things to talk about at dinner; people would rather relive golden boy Torres's stunning goal at the Euros than bring up Bai Feili's dream of grad school.
> Amid the clinking of glasses, it suddenly dawned on Bai Feili that Erniu was right. Truly outstanding students would never stay at their own school for grad school. Even if he got into Tsinghua's grad program, he'd still have lost in the end.


Everyone thinks they're unique, and only children especially so. Maybe when I was little I also thought there were winners and losers, but as I grew up I came to feel that life has no winning or losing. It's more of a journey, and what I care about is the scenery along the way.

> The chubby kid wiped the grains of rice stuck to his mouth with the back of his hand, took another sip of soup, and said, of course I can understand him, he's speaking the dialect of XX County in our Jiangsu Province!
> When students pressed the teacher on where this line of thinking actually came from, and why this method was used to solve the problem, the teacher would just cough and repeat the same line: "You just think about it, and then it comes out!"

In my four years of college I met all kinds of teachers. But because our school had no pull with high-caliber faculty, some of the teachers who came to us had previously only taught at third-tier colleges, and they were required to teach in English. You can imagine what torture that was for students like me who couldn't get used to Chinese-accented or otherwise mixed-accent English. It got to the point that starting around sophomore year I basically stopped going to class, because just like every other Chinese university, exams had a defined scope, and that scope came from past years' questions, so if you worked through the last few years' exams you could usually get a high score. But once in a while there was an even more outlandish teacher who, because of their own limited ability, would mark correct answers as wrong and take wrong ones as right. And because of school policy, students weren't allowed to challenge teachers like that. Then again, given China's total population, having surplus people in administration or at schools may be one way to relieve pressure. From what I know, a lot of universities have started expanding enrollment so that more young people have something to do, rather than ending up like those in Hong Kong who joined the street violence. There are many problems with teachers. Even someone like me, who really doesn't like dealing with other people, has had to stand up and call out to others, the higher-ups, or classmates when my core interests were harmed, to prove my case or present evidence. But in reality, even when I had plenty of evidence that I was right and the "teacher" was wrong, the school had all kinds of excuses to brush it off, especially when you don't have anyone "up top." I just don't know whether this is unique to China or whether other places in the world are like this too.

> He hadn't expected the library to be so crowded on a Saturday. Most of the tables were full. Bai Feili ran all over the library, up and down, and finally found a big empty table in a corner. The table normally sat four, six if you squeezed. Bai Feili sat at one corner and began laboriously reading his advanced math textbook.
> Just as Bai Feili was painfully matching wits with Cauchy, Lagrange, and L'Hôpital, a group of people walked over and quickly took over the table where he'd been sitting alone.
> By now the table was packed with seven people. With people already sitting on both his left and right, Bai Feili started to feel uncomfortable, but he figured the library was always crowded, so he put up with it, suppressing his boundless irritation and burying his head in his book to derive formulas.
> But before long, three or four more people came, carrying stools and sitting around the table, and they started talking to each other in low voices. Bai Feili's irritation had escalated into anger. He turned to the person talking behind him and said, excuse me, please keep quiet in the library.
> To his surprise, the guy acted as if he didn't even see Bai Feili and kept talking to the others, and even more people joined the discussion. Only then did Bai Feili realize these were journalism students who had just finished a social survey and come to the library for a group discussion. The thing is, even claiming seats should be first come, first served. You can't do whatever you want just because there are more of you!
> But the facts told Bai Feili that in the library, having more people really does mean you can do whatever you want, at least when it comes to claiming seats. In the end, Bai Feili had no choice but to pack up his advanced math book, yell "Lagrange!" at the group, and storm out of the library, vowing never again to set foot in such a den of iniquity.
> Walking down the road, Bai Feili was fuming and furious, and out of habit he repeated his famous saying: "A bottom-of-the-barrel 985 is just a bottom-of-the-barrel 985, a bunch of trash students and losers!"

This passage is especially vivid. Seat-claiming is a distinctive phenomenon too. But I think schools always have some "little-known" corners where students can go to study on their own.

> But how to escape, and what even counted as escaping, those were the detailed questions that kept bugging Bai Feili. So for several months after the semester started, Bai Feili fell into a loop: whenever he felt things weren't going well, he'd think of his Great Escape plan; thinking of the plan meant thinking about how to carry it out; thinking about how to carry it out drew a complete blank; the more he thought, the more his head hurt; the more he thought, the more irritated he got; once irritated, he'd angrily stop thinking about it; once he stopped thinking, he felt how great it was to not have to think about anything; the great days were always short, and soon some little thing would make him feel bad again; feeling bad, he'd think of his Great Escape plan... ...

I've never had the protagonist's experience of pulling off a smooth great escape, but I've also fallen into a similar loop and wasted time for nothing. I've also known what it's like, as the protagonist does, when your grades aren't as good as you expected, falling from a so-called "god" to a "loser." But later you numb yourself with the Ah Q spirit: "There's always someone worse than me," or the protagonist's "Just wait, next semester" mindset. My grades got worse every semester starting in sophomore year, until I went abroad for a year in the first half of senior year and came back. But unlike the protagonist, even though copying was rampant in China, I never thought about copying. My attitude was usually that if I was going to copy, I might as well not do it at all, so later I often missed a lot of deadlines, because I took time less and less seriously. They say slow work makes fine work, but in reality, more often it was just procrastination. I really like a song by MKJ, [Time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fwcYuL6Z74E), which says `In the end, you may falter, but rest assured time will not` (the song is mainly about how important time is; give it a listen if you're interested)

> Bai Feili chimed in, it's been two years, haven't you noticed? Pretty much all the teachers at our school are at this level. Either they speak Jiangsu county dialect, or they're blackboard-writing masters, or they're the "you just think about it" type. Why do you think I failed that mysterious subject freshman year? It's because that teacher was basically the "you just think about it" type too. She always avoided the points I didn't get. In class she just read from the textbook, and in Q&A sessions she just read from the answer key.

In the protagonist's major, some people transferred out and some transferred in. Though it's now called a "pit" major, it's also the school's flagship. Looking back on my own college major, I feel that picking what you think is good may really be worse than picking the school's flagship. At the very least, the admissions outcomes are on a different level. For example, biology sends one person to Harvard every year, while computer science gets at most into the Ivies. Some will say computer science has good job prospects, but when everyone thinks it's good, the bubble is already huge. Especially with the number of students in the major growing exponentially every year, low-skilled people will inevitably fail to find jobs, while for highly skilled talent the competitive pressure skyrockets.

> The advanced math teacher went on happily telling jokes about older graduates retaking exams all the way until the end of class. This time Bai Feili actually understood all of it, because he'd originally assumed the teacher would point out the key topics before the exam, so he'd specially arranged it in advance with the chubby kid from Jiangsu: he spent a hundred yuan treating the chubby kid to a small barbecue meal, then went to class with him and sat next to him, having the chubby kid do simultaneous interpretation for him.
> Unexpectedly, the hundred yuan bought him nothing. Near the end of class, Bai Feili couldn't hold back any longer and raised his hand to ask the advanced math teacher, aren't there any key topics for this exam?
> The advanced math teacher waved his big hand, pointed at the textbook, and said, everything I've taught is a key topic. Whatever's in the textbook that I haven't covered, you don't need to read! Don't worry, it definitely won't be on the exam!

> Bai Feili ran alone to the internet café at the school gate, opened Winning Eleven 8 on a computer, picked the fairly easy two-star difficulty, and used Brazil to crush China twenty games in a row, thirty to nothing, before he finally cooled off a little.

I smiled knowingly when I read this. Back when I played FIFA, I'd also use strong teams against weak ones, like an all-powerful god against ordinary people. Put crudely and simply, it just feels "great." But maybe it's like Wang Xiaobo's famous line: "All human suffering is, at its core, anger at one's own incompetence."

> At the gathering, Bai Feili learned that among his middle school classmates, only four or five had gotten into a 985 university in the first year's gaokao, and fewer than twenty had made the first-tier cutoff. Of those who retook it this year, just one got into a 211.
> Seeing the envious look from Wang Dapeng, who back in ninth grade had been neck and neck with him in grades, never able to pull ahead, Bai Feili felt hugely satisfied. After all, this kid had retaken the exam for another year and only got into a non-985, non-211 university in some godforsaken city in the northeast. Thinking about how the gap between himself and this dimwit in front of him would only keep widening, Bai Feili couldn't help laughing inside.
> After confirming that at least a third of the class would fail this exam along with him, Bai Feili went back to the dorm, lay on his bed, and felt slightly better.


Because I went abroad on an exchange in high school, I never attended the post-gaokao reunions with my elementary or middle school classmates. On one hand it's a bit of a shame, on the other I'm a bit relieved. I know some ended up somewhere better than me and some somewhere worse, but so what? I don't get satisfaction from comparing myself to others. But then, next:

> While chatting, Bai Feili learned that Wang Dapeng, who last year had gotten into that non-985, non-211 university in the northeast, decided a month after school started that the environment was terrible. Mainly, he neither liked nor could learn the major, one of the three "pit" majors. So the more he thought about it, the less willing he was to accept it, and in a fit of anger Wang Dapeng dropped out and went back to high school in October to retake the exam again.
> After this year's gaokao, he got straight into a Top 5 university.
> Getting into a good university paid off double. One part was moving up to a better tier of school; the other was that Chen Xiaoli, whom he'd chased for N years without success, finally agreed to be with him after finding out he'd gotten into a Top 5 university.
> Hearing this, the crispy fried pork in Bai Feili's mouth suddenly lost all its flavor. He pushed aside the vegetables he'd been about to keep dipping in the hot pot while shooting the breeze, pulled fifty yuan out of his pocket and put it on the table, said something had suddenly come up at home that afternoon so he wouldn't go to karaoke, and left without looking back.

Gloating over others' misfortune is not a good look.

> Liang Junjie walked with his head down, thinking that a lab genius like himself had sunk to the point of relying on someone else's stuff to pull the wool over people's eyes and scrape by. It was a huge humiliation. The more he thought, the more irritated he got, and he yelled "Get lost" at Bai Feili three times, telling Bai Feili never to do anything with him ever again! Never!
> Thinking of this, Bai Feili decided to start reading the lab manual properly from the beginning, vowing to master every lab procedure so that in future lab classes he'd never have to beg anyone, and instead others would be begging to cling to him!

I also find asking others for help a real hassle. Better to handle things myself.

> So the textbook was just for show. Even what the teacher said in class was just going through the motions. In the end, the scope of the exam was what really mattered. Figuring out what would be tested, what wouldn't, and the style of the exam was the key to doing well in this kind of "pit" major's specialty courses.

All exams are like that, aren't they? After all, an exam is only a subset of the knowledge.

> On his way back to the dorm with his backpack, Bai Feili deliberately took a detour past the little café at the school gate. Glancing through the window, he saw Brother Sheng and Xiaoyun from his class sitting at a round table, all lovey-dovey. Bai Feili turned his head and sighed as he walked, muttering to himself, the one good-looking girl in the class, and Sheng the punk got her.
> After standing there for a while, Bai Feili went downstairs alone and snuck up to Brother Sheng's dorm door. He crouched beside it pretending to be on the phone, one ear listening to what was going on inside. All evening he heard Brother Sheng bragging to his roommates about his glorious exploits of the past couple of days. After listening for over ten minutes, Bai Feili felt even more heartache. He hurried outside and ran laps on the sports field alone with his head down, venting his frustration. He didn't understand why he, a mere spectator, felt more and more heartbroken the more he watched, as if he were the one who'd been dumped.

Fortune favors the bold, hahaha.

> The salesperson said, our all-inclusive service originally costs ninety-eight thousand, but we're running a promotion right now, so it's only forty-nine thousand eight hundred. And if you sign today, we'll throw in ten free TOEFL classes.
> Stunned by the price, Bai Feili pretended to be calm and replied, I'll talk it over with my family and get back to you in a couple of days.
> Walking out with the plan in hand, Bai Feili steadied his racing heart and said mockingly, I've already got the plan, I can just DIY it myself. Why would I need someone to babysit me and nag me about my progress!
> After class, Bai Feili angrily went to the front desk and canceled the TOEFL class. Holding one thousand eight hundred in cash, Bai Feili thought, what couldn't I do with this one thousand eight hundred? Eating at the Jinqianbao buffet every day for a week, wouldn't that be great?

To be honest: study-abroad agencies and all sorts of prep classes really don't help much.

> In the end, Bai Feili had to pray along with a group of people to close out this "pleasant" dinner, then watched as the Korean guy left with two junior-college girls.

It feels like a hint at the "concubine-selection" scandal involving international students at Shan X University.

> As the dean in charge of teaching, I could have given you a very official answer: our college handles everything according to procedure, we haven't yet found anything abnormal in the situation you reported, and no action is needed!

People who accomplish great things have a vision far, far bigger than yours right now. You really should reflect on yourself and improve your knowledge!

Very Chinese characteristics.

I've never seen this, since computer science doesn't need hands-on lab work.

> Teacher Shang said, any PhD at our school who publishes in Nature automatically stays on after graduating as a full professor, and gets that whole floor of labs in the building across the way! (What Bai Feili didn't know at the time was that the one Nature paper Teacher Shang had published was actually as second author.)

Everyone exaggerates their achievements. For example, my most-cited paper is also one where I'm second author.

> Teacher Zhao told Bai Feili not to bother trying other labs either, saying, our college has plenty of grad student labor, on average two or three grad students per fume hood. We're not short of hands at all, but lab space is always tight. Many teachers think undergrads get in the way and don't want them at all, and now that they have to deal with this program, they're even less willing to take them, unless you're a senior who's confirmed to be recommended into grad school in that same lab.

Indeed, because China has a huge population, universities have lots of people too, but the number of students one teacher can take on is limited, so many people may not be able to join a lab. I felt this deeply during a week at Tsinghua: one meeting lasted six hours straight, with over 30 students ranging from undergrads to PhDs. But there's nothing to be done about it. Too many monks and not enough porridge. So if you want to avoid this situation, maybe going abroad is the only way.


> He told Bai Feili, don't listen to the old stories from some teachers at Old Western. They always say that as long as you're not from Tsinghua or Peking University, your school's name is pretty much the same to foreigners, that foreigners can't tell the difference between Shandong University and Shanxi University. In reality, as more and more Chinese students have gone abroad in recent years, nearly every top-fifty university in the US has someone in admissions who knows Chinese universities inside out. They not only know the difference between Shandong University and Shanxi University, they even know the difference between Jinan University (暨南) and University of Jinan (济南).

Chinese people compare foreign university rankings, so why wouldn't foreigners compare Chinese university rankings? I think this kind of "knowledge" should be common sense, but it's true some people have given up thinking and just take whatever others say as gospel. They never take the initiative to think through problems or try to solve them, or even when someone tells them the answer, they don't believe it.

> To recapture that feeling, Bai Feili even made a special trip to the stationery store at the gate of his old middle school and found the same notebook and gel pen he'd bought back then to write his plans. When he got back, he neatly wrote the name of this plan on the first page of the notebook: "I'm going to publish in Nature!"
> The very next day, Bai Feili tore the "I'm going to publish in Nature" plan he'd written over winter break to shreds. He decided to stop going to the lab to grind out papers for now, and instead focus on doing well on the GT and raising his GPA, aiming to get into a state university too.

The grand ideals at the start all end up as just talk, and he accepts that he can only be a "loser." "This is good enough." Of course, as an onlooker, I think there's nothing wrong with that, but watching the protagonist tumble down the hill like this still makes me sigh.

> Bai Feili immediately rolled his eyes and said, aren't those diploma-mill master's programs in the Commonwealth just pay-and-you're-in? What's there to admire? And plenty of them will definitely be much better known than hers!

I've also had thoughts of blaming my parents, but later I felt it was pointless. Better to just get out of there, or take out a loan / earn money myself.

> Bai Feili thought to himself, taking the grad entrance exam for your own school, how unambitious. Only idiots take the exam for their own school, and he still says he's great. I think he's just another slacker, coasting until graduation and then begging his boss for a spot to stay on.

I thought that way at first too, but later I felt, what's wrong with some classmates going to grad school at their own school? People have inertia. Some feel their current situation is already the best, and they gain confidence through comparison.

> Handsome Brother said, who are you going to blame for being so out of the loop yourself? You go around all day acting like a lone ranger, there's way more you don't know! Go back and reflect on yourself. See if you can change. If you can, change. If you can't, forget it, and then turn off your curiosity right away and stop asking around about this and that all day.

If you've chosen to be a "lone ranger," don't turn around and be a "gossip."

> At this point, the uncle sitting on the other side spoke up too: that's right, at the factory where I work, three new college graduates came in, and they're all assisting me, a technical-school graduate. He caved on the spot and immediately changed his position, from firmly refusing grad school and wanting to go out and work, to saying he'd go see what grad programs he could test into.

"Fear not a godlike opponent, fear a teammate who's dumb as a pig."

> After the exam, Bai Feili even went out of his way to stop a top student and ask how he'd studied for this course and why he could write so much. Nothing more to it than practice makes perfect!

> Bai Feili, who was reviewing his comprehensive advanced math book, shook his head as he read and said, when it comes to getting recommended for grad school, you can throw away your dignity, so what's a slap in the face! A fierce life is a shameless one, not afraid of being slapped in the face.

Everyone longs for a better university and a better life. I think life is unfair to begin with. What we need to do is make good use of the resources we already have and then go explore the unknown world. There's no need to care too much about how good or bad your grades are at any given moment. Keep an even mind, don't be elated by external gains or saddened by personal losses. Reading more books and thinking independently will be a big help in your future life.
