---
title: "Memoir for High School in China"
---

This is a post looking back on my high school years in China.

I think I started high school in 2011. My score on the high school entrance exam was decent, somewhere around the top thousand-something in the city, and I got into a provincial key high school in my hometown (Hefei). With my entrance score I should have been able to get into one of the elite classes, but that year they added an extra placement exam, mostly on competition-level material. I had always just studied what was in the textbooks, and the competition stuff was completely beyond me, so I did terribly in math, physics and chemistry. In the end I only got into a regular class (a "parallel class"). My parents didn't pull any strings to get me into an elite class either. Maybe they just didn't know any "officials" in the first place. Meanwhile, a few classmates I knew got straight into the elite classes through their parents' connections, no exam needed. Looking back now, they all ended up at much better universities than I did, but then again, it was only because I was in a regular class that I got the chance to go to Finland on an exchange.

Back to the story. In my first year I was full of ambition and ran for class monitor right after school started. Looking at how I acted back then, it seems pretty dumb now. Sometimes when the class got too noisy I'd slam the desk to "intimidate" everyone, but still nobody listened to me. Maybe it was that experience that made me think "leading other people" is nonsense, and that the only person you can really manage is yourself. A so-called "leader" can only tell you what you should do; if you don't do it, there's nothing the "leader" can do about it. Our homeroom teacher back then was a fresh college graduate who couldn't even speak standard Mandarin properly, so the class was always pretty chaotic. Nobody really listened in class, and my own grades kept getting worse. But this teacher had one good thing about him: he passed on basically every school announcement to the students, which is how I found out about an opportunity to go abroad on an exchange. I remember my classmates in the elite classes had never even heard of it, because the veteran teachers in the elite classes decided based on experience which activities to announce (they figured going abroad would only hurt your gaokao and was pointless, so they didn't tell the students). Before going abroad, I also took part in a LEGO robotics competition, but that competition made me fully realize how fake a lot of competitions in China are. The robot I built seriously and carefully scored worse than the ready-made ones other people got through connections, and this kind of thing gets dressed up as "big hands holding little hands." To be fair, though, if your parents work in a related field, kids really do get influenced by it. But the award winners I knew back then all seemed like slackers to me: terrible grades, and they got into the elite classes and into university through connections.

After my first year I went to Finland. For the details, check out one of my Zhihu [answers](https://www.zhihu.com/question/23337120/answer/98325091).

After I came back, I went back to a regular class, and I had to start again from the second year (so I spent four years in high school, though **supposedly** an earlier exchange student came back and went straight into the third year, and then got into Tsinghua). I was on the science track, and most people in the class I joined had picked science, so most of them stayed in that class (classes with too uneven a split between humanities and science got broken up). Since I was new, I didn't know anyone, so on the first day I just sat anywhere, and the guy sitting next to me turned out to be dead last in the class. When I first came back, our homeroom teacher was an experienced veteran chemistry teacher, and he was pretty strict. But after teaching for a while he left because of a family emergency. The next homeroom teacher was my favorite teacher of all my time in high school in China: He Ping, who taught math. His way of teaching math was very philosophical. He often used examples from classical Chinese texts to explain mathematical ideas, which I found amazing. I still remember his "asking the Way of zero." The other students didn't really like him, though, because the class's average math score dropped compared to before. But that stretch was when my math grades were the best of my whole high school career. He was also a bit like me in that he was a silent observer. Back then I often ran alone on the track after school (five or six laps each time, about three kilometers). He saw me once, and afterward he had the whole class go running together, though in the end very few people actually ran. I feel like Mr. He's teaching style was maybe too detached from the ordinary, so it didn't really help the average student, but for me it was really inspiring.

I spent most of my second year in a daze. I felt like I could no longer fit back into the Chinese system. At one point I was even memorizing SAT vocabulary and doing SAT practice tests, hoping to study in the US later. But my parents didn't support any of these efforts. They weren't willing to pay for me to take the SAT in Hong Kong, and they also thought studying in the US would be way too expensive. I remember one time, when I was applying to NYU Shanghai, I needed to check my essay for grammar and citation issues. I found someone on Taobao who would run it through Grammarly for me for just 1 RMB (cheaper than the ping-pong balls my dad was always playing with), but since I didn't have a Taobao account yet, I asked my dad to buy it for me, and he flat-out refused, saying it was too expensive and unnecessary. In the end I had no choice but to get another relative to buy it for me. From then on I stopped expecting much from my parents.

Back to high school. At the time, the school moved third-year students to another campus, but it was way too far from my home, more than an hour by bike, and we had to be at school by 6:50 a.m. for morning self-study. If you were late they wouldn't let you in (you'd have to stand at the school gate as punishment until first period). Our class also got a new homeroom teacher, a middle-aged male math teacher. I remember him very clearly too, because he's probably the only teacher who ever physically punished me directly. I remember we still had classes during the summer break after second year, and one time I got a homework problem wrong. The teacher said something like, "How can you get a problem like this wrong, are you an idiot?", then twisted my ear and made me stand for a whole class period as punishment. I wasn't the only one who got made to stand or had their ear twisted, but this kind of thing hit me really hard. I could hardly believe that I, an 18-year-old adult, could still be treated so humiliatingly, just for getting one problem wrong. Maybe in China this kind of thing is still common and tolerated. I remember being furious. When I got home I started punching the sandbag, boxing, and I think I even smashed some stuff. Afterward I just felt really wronged. But it's things like this that left me with no sense of identification with China or Chinese people at all. I felt like these people's values and worldview were in a completely different dimension. There was no way I could stay here in the future and keep living alongside these people. Even though I still speak Chinese, that's more just a tradition than anything else. And yet, in the eyes of other students, this teacher was "actually" a good teacher, because he really did raise the class's average math score quite a bit. But for me, my math kept sliding the whole time, and I couldn't muster any interest in it.

It wasn't until about three weeks before the gaokao that I started to feel nervous about actually taking it, and only then did I start seriously doing practice papers and problems. It didn't help much, though. My strongest subject was, of all things, still English, but on the Anhui exam paper that didn't make much of a difference.

Maybe I'm not a typical person living in China, but I do think a lot of my experiences in China changed the course of my life. These days I'm more of an advocate for free online learning. I donate to organizations like Khan Academy so that more people who want to learn can directly access the best resources in the world and learn from the best teachers in the world, instead of being limited by the environment they happen to be in. Sometimes I feel like I clearly could have done more, but I was always held back by my environment. I hope that people in the future can make fuller use of the internet to change their own "fate."

That's all.
