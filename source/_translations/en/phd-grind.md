The [original text](http://pgbovine.net/PhD-memoir/pguo-PhD-grind.pdf) of The PhD Grind

I think this memoir by Philip Guo is really useful for anyone thinking about doing a PhD. He records what happened in each of his six years as a PhD student and then shares his own reflections, which gives you a sense of what daily life is like for a computer science PhD student.

First off, Philip did both his undergrad and master's at MIT, then went to Stanford for his PhD on NDSEG and NSF fellowships. That's a very strong background. In Philip's words, he paid his dues: because he had a solid foundation early on (a top undergrad school, fellowships), he had the freedom not to work on the topics his advisor cared most about, since he wasn't taking money from his advisor's projects. But even with a background that strong, his research path was far from smooth.

His advisor at Stanford was Dawson, the author of Klee, the well-known bug-finding tool, and Philip spent most of his first three years contributing to Klee. But because his advisor and his senior labmate Cristi were extremely good and had already pushed Klee to a very high level, improving on it further was really hard. How hard? Besides Cristi, two other PhD students had been stuck on it: one went nine years without a paper and had to drop out, and the other also took nine years to barely publish one paper and graduate. Seeing that, I feel sorry for those two long-time PhD students, because anyone admitted to Stanford must already be a top student, but since this direction and these topics were already so mature, all their later work could do was patch things up and add small enhancements or features. In the end it was just a pity-graduate, and their chances of landing a faculty job were pretty slim. For the boss, since Dawson already had tenure, he could accept it if some of his attempts failed, but for a student, that one attempt might be their entire PhD lifecycle. So unless you have your own funding, it's better not to dive straight into a super hard project. That said, I haven't applied for a PhD yet, so this is all just armchair talk for now.

The turning point in Philip's research career came during his internship at Microsoft Research. He met some great researchers from other schools, and because Microsoft had real user data, there was a data moat, which really helps for publishing at top-tier conferences: the data is real and convincing, and since it only exists inside Microsoft, nobody else can compete with you on that front. I know this firsthand. Here at Zhejiang University, professors also care a lot about working with companies, partly to get exclusive data and partly to pave the way for students' jobs. One more advantage of Chinese universities, though (or maybe it's just what I happened to run into), is that buying certain sensitive equipment is easier than it is for companies.

After reading Philip's Grind and thinking back on my past year of research at Nanyang Technological University and Zhejiang University, I feel a bit ashamed. My communication with my advisor felt pretty inefficient, and I didn't get much done myself; it was mostly reading papers and tuning hyperparameters. Here's a summary of the specific problems I can remember and possible fixes:

1. Meetings often had no clear topic: when I first got there I didn't know what to work on. My advisor had some ideas, like some proofs for Bayesian problems, but they didn't feel like a good fit for me. A lot of the time it felt like awkward small talk. Later, though, my advisor said something in the WeChat group that I thought made a lot of sense: in meetings we should discuss what to do this week/next week, not summarize last week. The summary of last week should be sent to the advisor beforehand.
2. Not keeping records: I think one big problem I had before was repeating work. Because I didn't write down an experiment after running it, I might later test the same method again, which is really inefficient, and I kept making mistakes in the same places. Later on, thanks to my teammate mengyao, who wrote down the parameters she'd tried, both to make reporting easier and to avoid duplicate work, so she always knew exactly what she'd done, I quickly picked up the habit too.

I was visiting the College of Control Science at Zhejiang University. The more senior PhD students there still work on traditional control theory, and a lot of it doesn't involve "cutting-edge" stuff like machine learning. At first I kind of looked down on it and thought it wasn't cool, but now I think the senior PhD students are really impressive. Even though their topics aren't close to mine, they've already been through the grind and know their fields inside out, and they often give me a lot of insight, like which interesting problems are worth researching.

I talked with Professor Baohua before, and he said there are two kinds of PhD: one focuses on improving algorithms, the other on designing architectures. I feel like I want to do the second kind, systems, because I think architectural improvements have more impact and are more challenging. But right now I feel that early on I should start simple, begin with easy things and solve small problems first, and then in the middle and later stages of the PhD (if I do one) tackle hard problems such as system design.
