I actually started following Li Xiaolai a long time ago. I first heard of him in 2017 when I was investing in crypto; he was super famous back then (the "Marquis of Ten Thousand Coins"). Now he has retired on a high note, but his influence is still there. I noticed his github still gets updated pretty often, he writes jekyll and then uses all kinds of docs, which is pretty amazing.

Another fun anecdote: I heard that when he orders food, he'll order several portions of the same dish, just because that dish is tasty.

--- 
 back to the book

 I first saw this book on x, and someone had attached a cloud-drive link, so I downloaded it and didn't buy a physical copy. But the free copy had lots of typos. I found several versions and they were all the same, so it was a real struggle to read, and I feel it may differ quite a bit from what the author meant. But a lot of the text is pointless to read anyway (similar to scrolling Twitter or Xiaohongshu). I think the most valuable part of this book is its ranking of important values: **sleep, exercise, relaxation >>> work and study**, with work even coming after study. I think this really is the mainstream lately, or rather the change after people updated their thinking. People used to think work was what mattered, but the new idea now is that sleep matters most. It's not just bryan johnson or others doing longevity-type stuff; everyone is starting to pay more attention to their own physical condition, and work actually matters less. This trend is even more obvious from the post-95 generation to gen z. -> I think this is a good thing; people need to put more of their attention on themselves.

The book also covers a lot about dopamine, endorphins, and oxytocin, but it all feels like old news. If you've read about this stuff before, it feels very repetitive, but if you haven't, it might be somewhat novel.

As for attention, it's true that everyone is drowning in short videos, images, and text these days, but without them you'd feel bored. Nowadays at dinner gatherings, when there's nothing to talk about, people just play on their phones; when you don't want to talk to the kids, you play on your phone. I noticed that when my wife gets home, if she isn't talking she's on her phone. But when you actually put the phone down to talk, it feels like there's nothing to say, so you might as well hold your phone to avoid the awkwardness. Same thing in elevators or when waiting around with lots of people.

As for how to manage your own attention, I still think Balaji's time-management approach from before is useful, or learning from exercise or intermittent fasting: set up a context window, like this hour is for doing this one thing, or within a certain time block you do things a certain way. That should make it easier to manage.


I'll read more of Li Xiaolai's other books/blogs too, and if I have new thoughts I'll update them on this blog or on [x](https://x.com/dexhunter).
After all, the trend now is 
*you are your media*,
everyone is starting to be their own media.
