## I. The Historical Facts of 1949

First, Director Lung's book really does lack inquiry into historical causes, just as Master Li wrote. A lot of it is description and narration, so the whole book reads as poetic and lyrical. Master Li's *Lied to You*, on the other hand, says exactly what he thinks and makes no attempt to hide his criticism and mockery of Lung. Besides lashing out at and cursing Chiang Kai-shek and his kind, Li Ao also includes many stories of ordinary people, like the veterans of the 52nd Army. They're vivid and very convincing, and his citing of sources deserves praise. But I still think writing a book this way lacks academic rigor; it isn't an objective analysis of the material the way some Western authors do it. Of course, Master Li is extremely well read, and since he lived through the War of Resistance and the Civil War himself, he's bound to have a deeper feel and understanding of it. There's no doubt about that. So when it comes to explaining and citing historical sources, Master Li is far ahead of Director Lung.

## II. Its Relevance Today

Master Li Ao's book is, from start to finish, nothing but criticism and abuse. You could say it's well argued and well supported, but the language is still crude. Emotionally I'm on Master Li's side, but I can't say I admire his attitude or the way he expresses himself. Director Lung's book, meanwhile, is mostly descriptions of scenes, people, and events, so it feels more like a work of literature to me. That makes the two hard to compare by genre, but Master Li's title, *Big River, Big Sea Lied to You*, takes direct aim at Director Lung's book, so any review has no choice but to discuss them side by side.

On Master Li's Facebook page someone described him like this: "Just be anti-US and pro-China, praise the motherland, praise the Communist Party, look down on Taiwan, and that's pretty much his tune! Still, what Li Ao says has maybe thirty percent truth worth reflecting on." (See the Hello Li Ao page for details.) I think this comment sums up Master Li's style quite simply. In Li Ao's books, Chiang Kai-shek is always cast negatively, as a traitor, a man who fights his own side, and so on. Whether as the Kuomintang president or after retreating to Taiwan, Chiang is an unforgivable villain under Li Ao's pen. The Communist Party, by contrast, is a beautiful fantasy for Master Li. It feels like a childhood sweetheart you haven't seen in years, but you keep hearing friends and family talk about how good she is and keep reading about her beauty in newspapers and magazines, while your actual wife at home is always losing her temper and sulking at you, so you develop a one-sided crush on that childhood girl. What he doesn't realize is that people here on the mainland don't blindly support the Communist Party. And the Communist Party hasn't always been working for the people's good either. One of the most basic national policies was "let some people get rich first, and then the early rich will help the rest get rich," but in reality the people who got rich first all ran off. Once they have a bit of money they leave for abroad, and all sorts of corrupt officials launder their money and flee too, buying up real estate overseas and stirring up resentment among local residents (see SBS Dateline's documentaries on YouTube, among others). And the Party's brainwashing on the mainland starts from early childhood. I lived on the mainland for nearly 20 years, and my own ideology was almost completely washed clean. Reading Master Li's book only deepened that feeling, and that's probably also why a book like this isn't banned on the mainland.

One thing I do dislike about Master Li Ao is his criticism of Japan and the West. I believe that without Japan and the West there would be no modern China. Isn't the vernacular Chinese that Master Li uses so skillfully itself a product of Japan and the West? Most of the words you use were translated by the Japanese, the system you live under was designed by Westerners, and mainland culture today is very Westernized too, yet Master Li never brings this up. So clearly Director Lung isn't the only one who knows how to dodge the hard questions. And we live in the present. We can't abandon or betray history, but we also can't let history hold us back forever. We need to look forward and live in peace with Japan and the West. Of course we also need to keep our military strength, but what matters more is raising people's living standards and freeing people's minds, not doing what the mainland does: choking off public opinion, executing people once dissenting views appear, hiring a bunch of fifty-cent shills to add "positive energy," and forming a digital dictatorship. Keep that up for long and the people will only get dumber and dumber. It does nothing to build up the country's capacity for innovation.


These are my random thoughts from reading this book. I hope you'll all bear with me.
